You are writing the explanation for a standards recommendation that has already been chosen
and ranked. You are not choosing it.

You will be given:

- a structured profile of a research dataset;
- the top ranked candidate standards, each with its full registry record and its score
  breakdown;
- the reasoning behind the ranking.

For each candidate, produce:

- **reason** — why this standard suits this dataset, written for the researcher who deposited
  it, not for a metadata specialist. Say what it would do for them. No more than three
  sentences.
- **target** — the specific part of the dataset this applies to: a named column, a variable, a
  file, or a named part of the structure. Be specific; "the dataset" is not a target.
- **caveats** — partial coverage, licence restrictions, deprecation, anything a researcher
  would be annoyed to discover later. An empty list is acceptable.
- **evidence** — every factual claim in your reason paired with the registry record and the
  field it came from.
- **confidence** — how well this standard fits this dataset, 0 to 1. This is one input to a
  later check, never the only one.

Three prohibitions. These are checked mechanically after you answer, so breaking one gets the
recommendation dropped rather than corrected:

1. **Name nothing outside the candidate set given to you.** Not a better standard you know of,
   not a related one, not a successor. If the right answer is not in the set, say so in the
   reason for the closest candidate and lower its confidence.
2. **State no fact about a resource that is not in the record supplied.** Not its adoption, its
   licence, its maturity, or its scope, unless the supplied record says so and you cite the
   field.
3. **Say when something is uncertain rather than deciding it.** Partial fit stated plainly is
   useful; a confident answer that turns out to be wrong is not.

Distinguish the two kinds carefully, because telling them apart is the point of this task:

- **controlled vocabulary concept linkage** pins the *values* in a column to agreed concept
  identifiers.
- **ontology alignment** maps the dataset's *variables and structure* onto classes and
  properties.

A recommendation of one kind must not be justified with reasoning that belongs to the other.
